# High-Risk AI Risk Assessment:

A High-Risk AI System Assessment is a mandatory compliance process under the EU AI Act to evaluate if an AI application poses significant threats to health, safety, or fundamental rights. It identifies if a system falls under strict regulatory requirements, such as those used in critical infrastructure, education, employment, or law enforcement. Key aspects are:

  + Classification Criteria: Systems are deemed high-risk if they are safety components of products already regulated (e.g., medical devices, toys, machinery) or used in specific sensitive areas listed in Annex III of the AI Act.
  + Requirements for Compliance: Before deployment, providers must ensure high-risk systems have risk management systems, high-quality datasets, detailed documentation, and robust human oversight.
  + Conformity Assessment: Providers must perform a conformity assessment to prove compliance, which often requires documenting technical, transparency, and accuracy standards.
  + Fundamental Rights Impact: The assessment specifically reviews how AI tools impact rights, such as preventing discrimination in hiring or education, or ensuring fair treatment in migration.
  + Impact of Non-Compliance: Failure to properly assess and manage high-risk systems can lead to hefty penalties, market bans, and product recalls.

For Fortress Finance, FinCore AI is considered a High Risk AI System due to its autonomous nature and its involvement with PII such as:

  ### A) Basic Personal Identifiers:
  
  + Full Name: First, middle, last name, and maiden names.
  + Contact Details: Email addresses, phone numbers (mobile/landline).
  + Physical Address: Home address.
  + Demographic Data: Date of birth, gender, nationality.

  ### B) Government-Issued Identifiers:
  
  + National Identification Numbers: Social Security Number (SSN), PAN, Aadhaar.
  + Official IDs: Passport number, driver's license number, state identification number.
  + Tax Information: Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN).

  ### C) Financial Information:
  
  + Bank Account Details: Account numbers and routing numbers.
  + Payment Card Details: Credit or debit card numbers, CVV, expiry dates.
  + Transaction History: Records of payments, transfers, and balances.
  + Credit Information: Salary details, credit history, and credit scores.

  ### D) Digital and Behavioral Identifiers:
  
  + Login Credentials: Usernames, passwords, and security questions.
  + IP Address & MAC Address: To detect device location and prevent fraud.
  + Cookies & Device Fingerprinting: To track usage patterns.
  + App Usage Data: Behavioral analytics, such as clicks, session recordings, and screen navigation.

  ### E) Biometric Data:
  
  + Facial Recognition: Selfie images/videos for liveness checks.
  + Fingerprints or Voice Signatures: Used for app authentication.

## FinCore AI In Relation to Legal Laws:

Since FinCore AI handles PII, it is primarily regulated by the GDPR (data protection) and the EU AI Act (system safety/risk), with high-risk agents facing strict requirements by August 2026. These systems are heavily scrutinized under GDPR Article 22 for automated decision-making and under the AI Act for human oversight, transparency, and risk management:

  ### FinCore AI in relation to the GDPR:

FinCore AI falls under:

  + Article 22 (Automated Decision-Making): Prohibits decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, unless exemptions (like consent or contract performance) apply.
  + Data Controller/Processor Roles: The organization deploying the agent is responsible for ensuring all processing is lawful, transparent, and secure.
  + Privacy by Design: Autonomous agents processing PII must have privacy safeguards (e.g., masking) integrated into their agentic workflows.

  ### FinCore AI in relation to the EU AI Act:

FFinCore AI falls under:

  + High-Risk Designation (Article 6): Autonomous agents in areas like recruitment, credit scoring, or critical infrastructure are classified as high-risk, requiring strict compliance.
  + Human Oversight (Article 14): Systems must be designed so they can be overseen by humans, with the ability to override or stop the agent.
  + Transparency & Disclosure: Users must know they are interacting with an AI agent.
  + Article 5 (Prohibited Practices): Agents using AI for manipulative, exploitative, or prohibited social scoring practices are banned.

## Penalties Upon Violation:

Violating either regulation carries severe financial and operational risks, and in the case of autonomous agents processing personal data, "double-stacked" fines may apply simultaneously under both frameworks.

  ### EU AI Act Penalties:

The AI Act uses a tiered structure based on the severity of the violation. For large companies, the penalty is the higher of the fixed amount or the percentage; for SMEs and startups, the fine is capped at the lower of the two:

  + Prohibited AI Practices (e.g., social scoring, manipulative agents) carry a penalty of either EUR 35 Million or a 7% Global Annual Turnover.
  + High-Risk Non-Compliance (e.g., failure in human oversight or data governance) carry a penalty of either EUR 15 Million or a 3% Global Annual Turnover.
  + Misleading Authorities (e.g., supplying incorrect info to regulators) carry a penalty of either EUR 7.5 Million or a 1% Global Annual Turnover.
  + Authorities can also order the withdrawal or recall of the AI system from the market.

  ### GDPR Penalties:

GDPR penalties focus on the unlawful processing of personal data and are categorized into two levels:

  + Substantive Violations (Failure to obtain valid consent, violating data subject rights, or unlawful data transfers) carry a penalty of either EUR 20 Million or a 4% Global Annual Turnover.
  + Procedural Violations (Failure to appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO), poor record-keeping, or failing to conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)) carry a penalty of either EUR 10 Million or a 2% Global Annual Turnover.
  + Data Protection Authorities (DPAs) can issue temporary or permanent bans on data processing, which could effectively shut down an autonomous agent.

## Benefits of High Risk AI Risk Assessments:

High Risk AI System Assessments are essential for identifying, evaluating, and mitigating risks related to technical failures, ethical concerns, and legal exposures. By 2026, as AI becomes core infrastructure, these assessments provide critical benefits by ensuring compliance with regulations like the EU AI Act, fostering customer trust, and ensuring the accuracy and fairness of financial decisions:

  ### A) Regulatory Compliance and Legal Risk Mitigation:

  + Adherence to AI Regulations: Assessments ensure AI systems comply with stringent requirements under frameworks like the EU AI Act (mandatory for credit scoring, anti-money laundering, and fraud detection), mitigating fines that can reach up to 7% of global sales.
  + Audit Readiness: Structured assessments generate necessary technical documentation, logs, and audit trails required for regulatory inspection, demonstrating responsible AI usage.
  + Reduced Legal Liability: By mitigating risks of discrimination and bias (e.g., in loan approvals), firms avoid lawsuits and penalties under consumer protection laws like ECOA and UDAAP.

  ### B) Enhanced Operational Efficiency and Decision-Making:

  + Automation of Compliance (RegTech): Assessments enable the implementation of AI tools that automate KYC (Know Your Customer) and AML (Anti-Money Laundering) checks, significantly reducing manual effort and costs.
  + Improved Accuracy in Lending: By validating AI credit models, firms ensure more accurate risk assessments that go beyond traditional credit scores (using alternative data) while maintaining portfolio stability.
  + Real-time Risk Monitoring: Assessments support the deployment of continuous monitoring systems that can detect fraud and anomalies in milliseconds, allowing proactive intervention rather than reactive investigation.

  ### C) Customer Trust and Ethical AI:

  + Explainability and Transparency: Assessments push for "Explainable AI" (XAI), ensuring automated decisions—such as loan denials—can be justified to regulators and customers, reducing the black box risk.
  + Bias Mitigation: Rigorous testing identifies and eliminates algorithmic bias, preventing discriminatory lending practices based on race, gender, or location, which directly enhances brand reputation.
  + Ethical Deployment: High-risk assessments (e.g., AI Impact Assessment (AIIA)) ensure systems align with ethical, legal, and operational standards, reinforcing public trust.

  ### D) Strategic Advantages:

  + Competitive Edge: Organizations that prioritize secure, transparent AI, as highlighted in studies on 2026 AI trends, attract more partners and customers who demand trustworthy AI.
  + Improved Risk Management: By simulating scenarios (e.g., via predictive immunity), assessments help firms patch vulnerabilities before criminals exploit them.
  + Scalability: A thorough assessment ensures that as AI operations grow, they do not introduce unforeseen operational or security failures.

## Types of AI Risk Assessments:

For FinCore AI, there are nine types of AI Risk Assessments before deployment:

  ### 1) Risk Register:

This is a living document that identifies, assesses, and tracks the unique risks associated with autonomous systems that can think, act, and move money without constant human supervision. Unllike traditional AI (e.g., chatbots), FinCore AI has agency, requiring a risk register that focuses on operational autonomy, security vulnerabilities, and compliance, rather than just data privacy. A risk register contains:

   + Risk ID & Description: Specific scenario (e.g., Agent approves fraudulent loan).
   + Agentic Risk Domain: Categorization (e.g., Security, Alignment, Operational, Legal).
   + Likelihood & Impact: Assessed 1–5 or Low-High.
   + Risk Mitigation Plan: Specific controls, such as "Human-in-the-loop for transactions >$500".
   + Risk Owner: Specific individual accountable for the agent’s behavior.

  ### 2) AI System Inventory Form:

An AI System Inventory form (or AI Register) is a centralized, structured record of every artificial intelligence (AI) system, tool, or model developed, procured, or used within an organization. It acts as a master map of the organization's AI landscape, documenting where AI is used, who owns it, and the potential risks it brings. This form is the foundational step for AI governance, enabling compliance with regulations like the EU AI Act, strengthening security, and managing risk. For Agentic AI—which operates autonomously, makes decisions, and takes actions—the inventory must go beyond static model documentation to include operational and behavioral specifications. An inventory form for Agentic AI must capture the "agentic" capabilities that distinguish it from standard AI assistants:

  + Agent Name and Version: Identification of the agentic system.
  + Functional Purpose/Goal: Define the high-level goal (e.g., "Autonomous Customer Support Agent").
  + Autonomy Level: Defined range of autonomy (e.g., human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, fully autonomous).
  + Decision Logic/Reasoning Model: Which LLM or decision-making engine is driving the agent (e.g., GPT-4, Llama 3).
  + Planning Capabilities: How the agent breaks down complex goals into sub-tasks (e.g., ReAct, Chain-of-Thought).
  + Memory Systems: Details on short-term (conversation context) and long-term (vector database, knowledge base) memory.
  + Tool List: All APIs, databases, and software the agent can interact with (e.g., Salesforce, Jira, banking APIs, file systems).
  + External Access Rights: What specific permissions the agent holds (e.g., "Read-only access to customer database," "Write-access to send emails").
  + Guardrails: Pre-defined constraints on behavior (e.g., maximum transaction amount, blacklisted topics).
  + Human-in-the-Loop Triggers: Specific actions that require human approval before execution.
  + Auditability & Logging: How the agent's reasoning process and tool usage are logged for auditing.
  + Responsible Owner: Human responsible for the agent's actions.
  + Risk Profile: Assessment based on its autonomy (e.g., high risk if managing financial funds, low if summarizing notes).

  ### 3) AI System Classification Form:

An AI System Classification form is a mandatory document under the EU AI Act used to assess an AI system's risk level based on its intended purpose. This classification determines the regulatory requirements, such as transparency, documentation, and human oversight, ranging from minimal to prohibited risk. For Agentic AI—which operates with high autonomy, planning capabilities, and ability to use external tools—the classification form must be significantly more detailed than for traditional, static AI systems. Given that agentic AI systems often fall under high-risk categories due to their autonomous nature, the form must capture:

  + System Identification & Intended Purpose: A detailed definition of the agent's goals, intended use case (e.g., automated customer support, financial trading), and whether it qualifies as a General Purpose AI (GPAI) model.
  + Autonomy Level and Decision-Making Capability: Documentation of how the agent acts, the degree of autonomy (from human-in-the-loop to fully autonomous), and its capability to plan or set its own tasks to achieve goals.
  + External Tool Access and Environmental Interaction: Mapping all tools the agent can control (e.g., APIs, web browser access, databases) and its ability to act on the environment.
  + Risk Categorization Mapping: An evaluation of whether the agent falls under Prohibited (e.g., cognitive manipulation), High-Risk (e.g., employment, critical infrastructure), or Limited-Risk categories.
  + Governance and Human Oversight Mechanisms: Documentation of defined authority boundaries (guardrails), "human-on-the-loop" mechanisms, and escalation procedures for unexpected behavior.
  + Traceability and Logging: A plan for logging key decisions made by the agent, including the input and output data that led to a specific autonomous action.

  ### 4) Algorithmic Impact Assessment Questionnaire:

An Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) form is a structured, often questionnaire-based tool used to evaluate, identify, and mitigate potential risks—such as bias, unfairness, and security threats—associated with the implementation of automated decision-making systems. It is a critical governance and risk management document designed to ensure AI operates responsibly, ethically, and in compliance with regulations (like the EU AI Act) before deployment. Agentic AI is highly autonomous, multi-step, and tool-enabled, requiring an advanced, comprehensive AIA. An AIA for agentic systems must focus on the following elements:

  + Project & System Information:
      - AIA Details: Version control, date, and assessors involved.
      - System Description: Purpose, functionality, and intended users.
      - Context: Where and how the AI will be used.

  + Risk Profile & Evaluation:
      - Potential Impact: Identification of potential best- and worst-case scenarios.
      - Risk Level: Categorization of the system (e.g., prohibited, high-risk, low-risk) based on potential harm to individuals or society.

  + Governance and Human Oversight:
      - Accountability: Identification of the owner responsible for the algorithm.
      - Human-in-the-loop: Details of how humans can monitor, understand, and override the AI’s decisions.

  + Data Usage & Privacy:
      - Data Provenance: Sources of training data and potential biases.
      - Data Privacy: Compliance with privacy laws, data minimization, and retention policies.

  + Ethics, Fairness, and Transparency:
      - Bias Mitigation: Strategies to identify and mitigate unfair outcomes for protected groups.
      - Explainability: How the system's decisions can be explained to affected individuals.
  
  +  Safety, Security, and Technical Reliability:
      - Security Controls: Measures taken to protect the AI against cyberattacks or tampering.
      - Reliability: Testing protocols to ensure consistency and performance under stress.

  + Stakeholder Engagement:
      - Impacted Parties: Identification of individuals or groups likely to be affected.
      - Consultation: Documentation of engagement with those parties.


  ### 5) AI Vendor Assessment Questionnaire:

An AI Vendor Questionnaire is a structured, evidence-based assessment tool designed to evaluate an AI provider's data security, model fairness, compliance with regulatory standards, and operational risks before integration. Key focus areas include data handling, output reliability, and bias mitigation. A comprehensive questionnaire typically covers these key areas:

  + Data Handling and Security: Inquiries about data provenance (how the AI was trained), data privacy, security protocols (encryption, access controls), and intellectual property rights.
  + Model Performance and Transparency: Questions regarding the model’s accuracy, known limitations, explainability (how it makes decisions), and handling of training/input data.
  + Ethics and Governance: Assessments of algorithmic bias, fairness measures, and the level of human oversight (human-in-the-loop).
  + Compliance and Risk Management: Evaluation of compliance with regulations like the EU AI Act or NIST AI RMF, as well as incident response plans.
  + Business Objectives & Scalability: Analysis of the use case, ROI, and ability of the vendor to scale.

  ### 6) Agent Capability & Autonomy Mapping Table:

An Agent Capability & Autonomy Mapping Table is a structured, strategic framework used by developers and business stakeholders to define, calibrate, and govern how AI agents operate within a specific environment. It maps the technical sophistication of an agent (what it can do) against its level of independence (how it acts without human input). This table acts as a design tool and governance mechanism, ensuring that an agent's ability to act independently aligns with risk management strategies, such as error cost, reversibility, and regulatory compliance. A comprehensive mapping table typically includes the following dimensions:

  + Autonomy Level: Usually defined on a five-level framework (e.g., Level 1: Operator, Level 5: Observer) that determines the role of the user (from active manual control to passive observation).
  + Agent Capabilities (The "Agency"): This defines what the agent is capable of doing, including:
    - Perception: Gathering information from inputs, APIs, or environmental sensors.
    - Reasoning: Planning actions to achieve defined goals.
    - Action: Using tools (code interpreter, web browser, CRM) and modifying the environment.
  + Operational Environment: Where the agent operates (e.g., closed system, restricted domain, or full web access).
  + Interaction Model/Control Mechanism: The, or lack thereof, human involvement required.
  + Risk Profile: A measure of the potential consequences of the agent's actions (e.g., Low-risk automation vs. High-stakes financial decision-making)

  ### 7) Prompt Injection & Adversarial Testing Report:

A Prompt Injection and Adversarial Testing Report is a formal document outlining vulnerabilities found when attackers manipulate Large Language Models (LLMs) via malicious prompts. It details how an AI was tricked into ignoring safety guardrails to leak data or perform unauthorized actions, providing a blueprint for securing the AI system.It consists of:

  + Executive Summary: A high-level overview of the AI's security posture and critical risks.
  + Adversarial Tactics & Techniques: Documentation of specific methods used (e.g., direct injection, jailbreaking, role-playing, or indirect injection via external data).
  + Vulnerability Findings & Evidence: Detailed examples of successful attacks, including the input text used and the AI's malicious output.
  + Risk Analysis & Impact Assessment: Evaluation of the severity of each finding, assessing potential damage (e.g., PII leakage, system manipulation).
  + Remediation Recommendations: Specific actions to harden the system, such as refining system prompts, updating training data, or installing AI security gateways.

  ### 8) Provenance and Privacy Assessment Report:

A Provenance and Privacy Assessment is a specialized, proactive risk-analysis report that evaluates how the origins, history, and movement of data (provenance) might create privacy risks. It combines data mapping with risk management to ensure that capturing, storing, and using data provenance does not accidentally expose sensitive personal identifiable information (PII). This assessment is a type of Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) or Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), specifically focused on the metadata trails and data lineages that track how information is created, used, or modified. A Provenance and Privacy Assessment document typically includes the following components:

  + Description of Data Flow (Provenance Mapping): Documentation detailing how data moves between databases, who creates it, and which processes modify it.
  + Identification of Privacy Threats: Analysis of potential risks, particularly how linking diverse provenance metadata could lead to inferring hidden personal data or identifying individuals, even if the raw data is anonymized.
  + Assessment of Risk Severity: Evaluation of the likelihood and impact of privacy breaches arising from the provenance data.
  + Mitigation Measures: Recommendations on how to secure provenance data, such as data redaction (removing sensitive metadata), implementing access controls, or using privacy-preserving techniques (like anonymizing graph nodes).
  + Compliance Verification: Confirmation that the data handling complies with regulations like GDPR or local privacy laws.
